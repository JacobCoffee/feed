AI and Irontown

For our last movie night we watched Princess Mononoke. I remembered loving that movie when I was younger, but watching it as an adult made me once again realize how Miyazaki has a way of expressing diverging sides without picking one. The film shows people, animals and spirits with legitimate and largely incompatible needs, and each faction is further engaged in infighting.

A central arc of the story is Lady Eboshi’s Irontown, which gives people who were largely rejected by society work, protection and dignity. She gives shelter and work to those who have leprosy and to former sex workers. The mine and the weapon factory provide them with a livelihood, but they also destroy the forest around them. The forest’s inhabitants, in particular the boars, response choose war against the humans to defend themselves. Eboshi is not portrayed as a villain. She does real good for the people of Irontown, but also is an arms manufacturer who is destroying the forest around it.

The film anchors around a character called Ashitaka, who has been condemned to death by an unfortunate encounter with a god that was turned into a demon after being fatally wounded by one of Eboshi’s guns. He doesn’t take sides, but he wants to understand and find the truth. Another character is San (whom they call Mononoke), a girl who was raised by wolves and, despite being human, considers herself part of the forest.

This movie’s backdrop is human progress, and it reminded me so much of the challenges we are facing today with AI. AI is useful, empowering and consequential beyond the people who benefit from it. It’s similar to the iron in the story. Irontown gives the outcast purpose and power. There are people today who see themselves in an environment just like that: people who were never let in before and who can now build things that were out of reach. They are wielding AI to their benefit. But there are negative externalities. In Mononoke it’s not just the spirits and animals in the forest who bear the consequences, it’s also other people. Irontown produces guns, and its people are engaged in fights with other humans. There is conflict and power seeking everywhere.

Another, unseen character is the power-hungry emperor, who wants the head of the forest spirit, a god, because he believes it will make him immortal. Eboshi is willing to support that cause, not because she thinks it’s right, but because she is at war with the forest’s gods who are preventing her expansion, so they have a common interest. But there really is no need for anyone to kill the forest spirit, in the same way there is no need for any one country to “win at AI”. What drives it is the fear of what happens if someone else wins first. And the emperor is willing to have a god killed for it. The people who help him with that desire, Eboshi included, don’t understand the chaos it will unleash.

There it also shows the dangers of seeking overwhelming power without comprehension of how to control its consequences. The pursuit of power itself becomes destructive. In the end, the gods die, and what grows back is not the old forest anymore. Irontown is destroyed. Eboshi wants to rebuild it, and to build a better town this time. Ashitaka stays with the people of Irontown after his curse is lifted by the dying forest spirit. San cannot forgive the humans and stays with the wolves. In the end, nothing is truly resolved, but there is a truce that came at great cost.

Ashitaka’s role in the movie is one of a neutral actor. He was wronged both by a spirit turned demon in the forest, who put a curse on him, and, indirectly, by Eboshi, who caused the spirit to turn evil. Yet he harbors no ill will towards either side. He sets out to “see with eyes unclouded by hate,” and in the course of the movie he is seen as an unaligned actor. He helps people on both sides, and stands in their way to prevent further conflict. He doesn’t feel like a pacifist, but he’s the closest to one there is. He’s a truth seeker in a changing world. Because he engages he’s seen as suspicious by both sides, and ends up shot by the people of Irontown when he tries to rescue San.

I use AI every day and find it genuinely empowering. But I also recognize the pain it causes some, and the moral problems it poses. It’s genuine progress for humanity, but it comes at significant cost. And many of those costs will be carried by people who aren’t going to benefit from it.

So it’s no surprise that AI feels like a threat to many today. Communities currently strongly align themselves with one side and fight against the other. The forest spirit did not have to die and the boars did not have to die in a senseless war.

The question is not whether there will be iron, but what kind of Irontown we build with it. I want our conversation about AI to make room for nuance and genuine understanding, for us to look at it and each other with eyes unclouded by hate. We should take advantage of its real benefits, but also recognize the damage it can do, and the choices we still have about what we build and what we’re willing to sacrifice for it. I like to think there is a path for people with differing opinions can find a way to live alongside before losing something we can’t get back.

Stable URLs for 42,000 Huts and Peaks

biwak.info is my map of Alpine huts, bivouacs and peaks. For a long time the only way to link to a hut was a #Name hash that did an exact name match and fell back to the search when the name wasn't unique. I wanted real URLs I can share and that show a proper preview card in a chat: biwak.info/p/matterhorn.

The data comes from OpenStreetMap: 9,429 huts and bivouacs and 33,437 named peaks, 42,866 places in total. Turning a name into a URL is easy. The hard part is that many places share a name, and that a URL, once shared, should keep pointing to the same place, even when the data changes underneath.

From a name to a slug

The first step is a plain slugify: German umlauts are written out, everything else is folded to ASCII, lowercased, and every run of other characters becomes a single dash.

GERMAN_TRANSLIT = str.maketrans({"ä": "ae", "ö": "oe", "ü": "ue", "Ä": "Ae", "Ö": "Oe", "Ü": "Ue",
                                 "ß": "ss", "ẞ": "ss"})


def slugify(text):
    """German transliteration for umlauts and ß, then ASCII-fold, lowercase, everything else to single dashes."""
    text = (text or "").translate(GERMAN_TRANSLIT)
    text = unicodedata.normalize("NFKD", text).encode("ascii", "ignore").decode("ascii").lower()
    return re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9]+", "-", text).strip("-")

So Oberaletschhütte becomes oberaletschhuette, Štuhecov dom becomes stuhecov-dom and Rifugio Città di Busto becomes rifugio-citta-di-busto. None of the 42,866 names ends up empty.

Names are not unique

Alpine names repeat a lot. Every valley has its Jagdhütte and its Rosskopf. With the name alone, the most common slugs are:

Slug Places
jagdhuette 70
schwarzkogel 37
buchberg 32
rosskopf 32
mitterberg 31

Before choosing a scheme, I measured how many places would still share a slug with different suffixes:

Slug made of Shared slugs Places affected Worst
name 2,181 6,690 (15.6 %) 70
name + country 1,993 5,936 (13.8 %) 64
name + region 1,516 3,932 (9.2 %) 27
name + region + elevation 118 394 (0.9 %) 21

Adding the country barely helps: most duplicates are in the same country. The region roughly halves them, but there are still 27 places called Jagdhütte in Carinthia alone. Elevation fixes most of the rest. It can't fix everything, because 4,311 of the huts have no elevation in OpenStreetMap. The worst case in the last row is casella in Liguria: 21 small huts with the same name and no elevation.

So there is no single suffix that always works, and adding all of them to every URL would make /p/matterhorn look like /p/matterhorn-wallis-4478.

Add a suffix only while the name is shared

The rule I settled on: a place gets the shortest slug that is unique, trying these in order:

  1. the plain name: /p/matterhorn
  2. name and region: /p/oberaletschhuette stays plain, but a shared name becomes something like /p/rosskopf-tirol
  3. name, region and elevation: /p/rosskopf-tirol-2257
  4. name, region and OpenStreetMap id: /p/jagdhuette-kaernten-w1202594655

In code this is a generator that yields the candidates for one place, given all current places with the same name slug:

def candidates(p, group):
    """Slugs for POI p in order of preference; `group` = all current POIs with the same name slug."""
    base, region = p["base"], slugify(p["region"])
    others = [o for o in group if o["id"] != p["id"]]
    if not others:
        yield base
    if not any(slugify(o["region"]) == region for o in others):
        yield f"{base}-{region}"
    if p["ele"] is not None and not any(slugify(o["region"]) == region and o["ele"] == p["ele"] for o in others):
        yield f"{base}-{region}-{p['ele']}"
    yield f"{base}-{region}-{p['id']}"

The OpenStreetMap id is the last resort because it's always unique, but nobody can read it. On today's data the 42,866 places split like this:

Slug Places
plain name 36,176
+ region 2,758
+ elevation 3,474
+ OpenStreetMap id 458

Regions from Natural Earth

The region has to be something people recognise. I cut the Natural Earth admin-1 boundaries down to the units that make sense in the Alps: the Bundesland in Austria and Germany, the Kanton in Switzerland, the département in France (Savoie and Haute-Savoie are better known than the big régions), the regione in Italy with South Tyrol and Trentino split, and the statistical regions in Slovenia. Names use the German form where that is common (Wallis, Tessin, Aostatal), otherwise the local one. The places on the map fall into 63 of these regions.

Each place gets its region by a point-in-polygon lookup with Shapely. The boundaries are simplified, so a hut right on a border or a lake shore can fall outside every polygon. Then the nearest polygon wins:

def lookup(self, lat, lon):
    """(cc, region name); points just outside every polygon (coast, simplified border) take the nearest."""
    pt = Point(lon, lat)
    for i in self.tree.query(pt):
        if self.geoms[i].contains(pt):
            break
    else:
        i = self.tree.nearest(pt)
    return self.props[i]["cc"], self.props[i]["name"]

The region is also shown on the preview card and in the detail view, next to the type: "Biwak · Haute-Savoie, Frankreich".

Stable: a registry that never forgets

The scheme above depends on the data. If someone adds a second Matterhorn to OpenStreetMap tomorrow, the plain rule would rename the real one to matterhorn-wallis, and every link shared so far would break.

So the build keeps a slug registry, data/slug-registry.json, which is committed to git. It maps every slug ever handed out to the OpenStreetMap id and the name:

"matterhorn": ["n26863664", "Matterhorn"],
"oberaletschhuette": ["n11262553552", "Oberaletschhütte"],

The assignment then follows three rules:

  • A slug in the registry never moves to another place.
  • A place keeps its registry slug as long as it still fits its current name, even if the plain rule would now give it a different one. When a second Matterhorn shows up, the old one stays matterhorn and only the newcomer gets a suffix.
  • A new or renamed place gets the first candidate that isn't taken yet. The old slug of a renamed place stays in the registry and redirects to the new one.
primary = {}
for p in pois:
    fitting = [s for s in by_id.get(p["id"], []) if s == p["base"] or s.startswith(p["base"] + "-")]
    if fitting:
        primary[p["id"]] = min(fitting, key=lambda s: (len(s), s))

for base in sorted(groups):
    for p in groups[base]:
        if p["id"] in primary:
            continue
        slug = next(s for s in candidates(p, groups[base]) if s not in registry)
        registry[slug] = [p["id"], p["name"]]
        primary[p["id"]] = slug

A place that disappears from OpenStreetMap keeps its slug reserved. Opening it starts a search for the old name instead of showing someone else's hut.

Because the registry is a sorted JSON file with one slug per line, a build that changes slugs shows up as a readable git diff. That made one mistake easy to spot: the first version folded ü to u, so the Oberaletschhütte was /p/oberaletschhutte. Two weeks after launch I switched to ue, which changed 7,182 slugs. I rebuilt the registry from scratch, so the old slugs are gone instead of redirecting.

Two fallbacks

A plain name that is shared, like /p/rosskopf, doesn't return an error. It opens the search with exactly the places of that name, with their type, elevation and region, and each of them links to its own slug. The same works one level down for /p/rosskopf-tirol.

A slug that ends in an OpenStreetMap id always resolves, even if the name part is wrong or outdated. /p/old-name-n11262553552 answers with a 301 to /p/oberaletschhuette.

Limits

  • Readable doesn't mean unique. 458 places end with an OpenStreetMap id in their slug. Better elevation data in OpenStreetMap would shrink that number, and the registry would still keep the old slugs working as redirects.
  • The registry only grows. It has one line per slug ever handed out, 42,866 today.
  • Changing the rules touches thousands of URLs. Any change to slugify or the regions renames a large part of the places, as the umlaut switch showed.

The same /p/ URLs also let the map open peaks directly, as described in Loading 33,000 Peaks on Demand with a z10 Tile Grid.

How the Python ecosystem responded to the eMudhra intermediate compromise

The news of the eMudhra Intermediate CA compromise was published on October 20th, 2026. Responding to an incident like a Certificate Authority (CA) compromise at an ecosystem-scale is far from “routine”; the assumption that HTTPS is secure is baked into many aspects of Python applications and packaging. This meant it was critical to mount a response that was both comprehensive and customized to each affected project and service, ranging from the Python language, pip, and the Python Package Index (PyPI).

This is where security staffing at the Python Software Foundation made a difference for Python. Staffing meant that Seth Larson, the Security Developer-in-Residence, was on standby able to respond and begin work on the issue immediately. Pre-existing relationships with affected project maintainers afforded plenty of trust, and the work was completed in a way that respected the time of volunteer maintainers for affected packages by reaching out proactively with clear next steps. Thanks to Alpha-Omega for funding the Security Developer-in-Residence position at the Python Software Foundation.

The incident was mitigated across multiple components within two days. Thanks to the work of their respective maintainers: fixes were made available for certifi, Python, pip, uv, sigstore, virtualenv, conda, pixi, manylinux, gh-action-pypi-publish, and many other projects. Vulnerable versions of certifi, pip, and sigstore were patched across all projects and services that the Python Software Foundation stewards, including PyPI, Python’s release process and build bots, and python.org. Containers have been rebuilt with the correct set of certificates.

Advisories, CVE records, banners, and discussion threads were created to alert the community about the work necessary to mitigate this incident and to be a location where relevant discussions and questions were answered by experts. Subscribe to the security-announce mailing list for future advisories about Python, pip, and other exceptional circumstances like this incident. Information about which projects are affected is available on discuss.python.org and around the web for more projects.

How did this incident affect Python as an ecosystem?

This section details the current situation of multiple Python projects. An aspect of positive security culture is “blameless post-portems”, we can only improve the future and the best way to do this is to show up and support the projects and maintainers that you depend on. Please note that all decisions below have historical context and sound reasons for being: Python is about the same age as TLS and is much older than the expectation that your system has up-to-date and secure CA certificate bundles.

The key component requiring a prompt response to the incident from the Python ecosystem is “certifi” and the advisory GHSA-6q48-6c7w-9gw2. Certifi is a repackaging of Mozilla’s CA bundle as a Python package and used by many popular projects including pip and requests. The package only includes root CAs, not intermediates, and therefore needed to remove the root CA from the CA bundle (“emSign Root TLS CA - G3”) to distrust all the impacted intermediate certificates.

Keeping a long story short: certifi is a critical dependency for being able to bootstrap a Python virtual environment into being able to install packages from PyPI. pip bundles its dependencies which includes “Requests” which depends on certifi. Python bundles pip so new virtual environments always contain pip through a mechanism called “ensurepip”.

This arrangement put all three of these components, Python, pip, and certifi, in the critical path for permanently remediating this incident as a user through software updates. There also isn’t an easy answer for older versions of Python which would permanently contain an older version of pip due to compatibility concerns. To address this concern, Python has added a “last-resort” denylist to reject the compromised intermediate certificates during a TLS handshake, fixed versions and more information tracked via CVE-2026-115557 which has been backported to older versions of Python.

What happens next?

We’re continuing our efforts to alert users through advisories and CVE and using banners on the PyPI and discuss.python.org and will be closely monitoring the uptake of patched versions of “certifi” across the ecosystem. The immediate, synchronous response has concluded, now we turn to what should be prioritized so a future incident like a CA compromise might not require as urgent a response from volunteer maintainers.

Right from the beginning of the incident it was identified that TLS handshakes made by browsers were secure, but tools like pip, git, uv weren’t secure. Why is this? Browsers were able to secure their certificate stores quickly and without requiring a software update by using Certificate Revocation Lists (CRL). CRLs allow providers of CAs to provide information to verifiers outside of the software update process, such as when certain certificates should no longer be used due to revocation or compromise.

These technologies may be integrated into Python in the future to provide a quicker and robust response to future incidents involving CA certificates and allow distributions to configure these facilities system-wide. One potential project to watch is upki, which has already been adopted by Ubuntu, provides certificate revocation functionality similar to that of browsers on platforms which previously did not offer such facilities.

Getting Started with Autonomous Development in Wing Pro 12.1

This Wing Tip describes how to get started with autonomous development in Wing Pro 12.1. You provide a high-level specification for a new application or a major feature. Wing's Tasks Manager fleshes it out, plans the work, splits it into tasks, and keeps a team of AI agents working on them for hours or days, as needed. The agents write the code, unit tests, and documentation, and commit their work in logical units. You answer questions, review results, and ask for revisions in the Tasks tool, which summarizes work done and decisions made.

Autonomous development shifts your focus from coding details to product design and oversight. You explain your vision. Your team of AI agents takes care of the implementation, end to end, far faster than you could develop the code on your own.

This Wing Tip is part of a series of Wing Tips on working with AI agents in Wing. For more detail on each step, see the How-To Autonomous Development with Wing Pro.

Getting Started

We recommend using a reasonably isolated environment, such as a virtual machine, when using AI agents. At the very least, prevent agents from accessing the keys to your server infrastructure, reading sensitive data, or pushing to your main repository.

(1) Install an AI agent and set up an account for it that covers the amount of work you expect to do. For sustained use, a subscription is generally much cheaper than paying for usage on an API key. Claude Code and Codex are best for autonomous development, since they check the safety of the commands they run. Antigravity, Copilot, and Cursor also work but don't provide the same level of safety. See Setting Up an AI Agent and AI Agent Safety for details.

(2) Install git or Mercurial, if you don't already have one of them. Wing uses it to track, review, and commit each task's changes. You don't need to learn how to use it, since Wing and the agents do that for you.

(3) Create a new project with New Project in the Project menu. Choose New directory and enter a Name, then choose your agent under AI Agent and a Python environment under Python. Leave Create a Git repository checked and click Create Project. Setting up the project for an agent turns on the Tasks Manager and lets it commit autonomously:

Shown above: Setting up a new project for an AI agent.

(4) Check the Tasks Manager's settings with the settings icon at the right of the Manager's bar, at the top of the Execute tab in the Tasks tool. The defaults are a good place to start. We recommend leaving automatic commits on, so work can go on while you are away. You can still look over every committed task on the Review tab and ask for changes.

Shown above: The Tasks Manager's settings.

(5) Write a specification or tasks on the Plan tab of the Tasks tool, and mark them Ready by clicking their Draft buttons. A task can be anything from a bug report to a high-level specification for a whole application. Describe what the code should do and any technical constraints that matter from the start. The Manager researches the rest, asks you questions, and writes out the tasks:

Shown above: A specification on the Plan tab, marked Ready.

You can watch progress on the Execute tab, and click a queue's name to see its session in the AI Agent tool. Problems that agents notice along the way, and follow-up tasks the Manager proposes, appear in the Incoming view on the Plan tab for you to make Ready or dismiss.

(6) Answer questions on the Questions tab when the Manager or a task needs your input. Other tasks keep running while it waits. You can also talk to the Manager directly on the Execute tab, to ask about progress or give it instructions such as "ask me first about anything that changes a file format."

Shown above: Questions about the specification, waiting on the Questions tab.

(7) Review finished work on the Review tab. Each task reports a summary of its changes and the decisions made in it. Use Revise to ask for changes to any decision or the task as a whole. If you are committing work yourself, use Start Review to view and comment on the code, or Commit to commit it without further review.

Shown above: A finished task's report on the Review tab, with automatic commits turned off.

Usage Tips

For better results, build tests and cross-checks into your process:

  • Ask the Manager early on to set up a way for agents to run your application as a user would, so they can see the results of their work.
  • Add tasks at key points that review the code for security flaws, gaps in test coverage, or anything else that matters to you. Problems they find automatically become new tasks.
  • Let the Manager commit on a separate branch, and review that branch before merging it into your main branch.
  • Use the application yourself, and add tasks for the bugs and improvements you find.

If you're unfamiliar with any of this, just tell the Manager what you need, in the Execute tab of the Tasks tool. It can help you set up your development environment and get started with autonomous software development.

Further Reading

See The Tasks Manager Agent in the Wing Pro manual for details. For examples of complete projects built this way, see the Autonomous Development Demos.

Unnecessary syntax in Python

Unnecessary syntax in Python isn't always unhelpful. An explicit return None, an else, a keyword argument, or a trailing comma can make your code more readable.

Table of contents

  1. Return None
  2. An else-return
  3. An else on a try
  4. Keyword arguments
  5. Unnecessary default values
  6. The dictionary keys method
  7. Explicit string concatenation
  8. Trailing commas
  9. Unnecessary variables
  10. Parentheses
  11. Whitespace
  12. Consider unnecessary syntax

Return None

The None in the return statement below isn't necessary:

def item_or_none(sequence, index):
    try:
        return sequence[index]
    except IndexError:
        return None

Using a bare return statement would have worked:

def item_or_none(sequence, index):
    try:
        return sequence[index]
    except IndexError:
        return

In fact, even no return at all would have worked:

def item_or_none(sequence, index):
    try:
        return sequence[index]
    except IndexError:
        pass

Functions return None by default in Python.

But I find return None more explicit and PEP 8 recommends return None in this case:

Be consistent in return statements. Either all return statements in a function should return an expression, or none of them should. If any return statement returns an expression, any return statements where no value is returned should explicitly state this as return None, and an explicit return statement should be present at the end of the function (if reachable).

Explicitly returning None makes it clear that we're returning None deliberately. The function's return value is significant, unlike most functions that simply return None by default.
