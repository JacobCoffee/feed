20 Years of Pyrolog

On November 12, 2006 I did the first surviving commit of my RPython based Prolog implementation Pyrolog into the PyPy repository. I had been working on it since spring 2006 already. In that year we were finishing up the first PyPy EU research project and I was finishing up my Bachelor studies at Uni Düsseldorf. Since it's now 20 years later I wanted to write a personal retrospective post on this project. Pyrolog never had any real users, but it still led to some interesting research and influenced a number of other RPython projects.

Context

As part of the EU project we had promised lots of things, including implementing some constraint programming in PyPy. For this strand of the work I learned Prolog by reading Clocksin and Mellish's Programming Prolog [CM2003] and later the Art of Prolog [SS1994].

In March 2006, the PyPy project partner Logilab organized a sprint in UCLouvain in Louvain-la-Neuve (Belgium), with Peter van Roy and some of the researchers working on Gecode. The plan was to add some kind of unification and constraint solving to PyPy. The Logilab people were quite inspired by the Van Roy/Haridi book [VH2004] and I later studied his Prolog implementation survey paper [VanRoy1994].

In 2006 I also attended my advisor Michael Leuschel's Prolog lectures in Düsseldorf. During that I started working on a Prolog interpreter in RPython, later called Pyrolog. Much of the early commit history is lost unfortunately, it didn't make the Subversion->Mercurial jump correctly and I don't know where the codespeak archives are now.

2006/2007 The first year

Pyrolog's first incarnation had a fairly straightforward implementation of unification in object-oriented RPython code. Logical variables stored an index, with their bindings stored in a global array at that index.

That version represented choice points by having exception handlers on the interpreter level, and backtracking via throwing exceptions, catching them further up. It used a continuation-based architecture for storing the calls left to do. Those success continuations were stored on the heap and continuously allocated and consumed. This approach was taken from Paul Tarau's BinProlog ([TB1990], [Tarau1992]) and also based on some other Prolog papers [Lindgren1994] [Neumerkel1995].

To deal with deep recursion that Prolog programs typically do, that version of Pyrolog relied on RPython's stackless transformation [BR2007D071], which would unwind the host stack (i.e. the C stack) and store its information on the host heap if host stack overflow was getting close. When storing C stack frames on the heap in this way, we also did some amount of tail call optimization. We were super into this idea at the time. However, it turned out not to be too practical, making the binary twice as large and slower, so eventually we removed it again.

I also experimented with using the first RPython JIT on that version of Pyrolog. That JIT was a partial-evaluation-based method JIT and managed to speed up some small Python programs. I don't think it did very much for Pyrolog though.

The performance of that version of Pyrolog was quite disappointing. In my B.Sc. thesis [Bolz2007] I did some measurements against SWI and Sicstus Prolog. Sicstus was often an order of magnitude faster than Pyrolog at this point.

In the thesis I also implemented a way to call into Prolog from Python code and had a case study where I implemented a Tic-Tac-Toe GUI in Python, with the game logic in Prolog.

Shortly after finishing my thesis, I changed the variable representation: instead of indexing into a global binding array, each variable object stored its own binding. The exception-based backtracking architecture stayed in place for another two years.

2008/2009 JIT developments on the PyPy side

After the EU project finished we started working on various new architectural ideas for the RPython meta-JIT. Armin Rigo, me and some others wrote two new prototypes. We weren't happy with them, so in 2008 Armin and I started experimenting with a prototype for a new trace-based JIT written in Prolog. We used Prolog because its pattern matching and ease of meta-programming allowed us to experiment more easily with different designs.

Towards the end of 2008 and throughout 2009 Armin, Maciej Fijałkowski, me and others started porting the design back to RPython and called it pyjitpl5 (the "pl" in the name stands for "Prolog"). This is still the base of the current PyPy meta-JIT compiler and became the subject of the second (smaller) PyPy EU project in the Eurostars program.

We eventually published a paper about the Prolog version of the JIT [BLR2010] at LOPSTR 2009.

2009-2010 New Pyrolog Architecture: More Continuations

After working mostly on PyPy and the new JIT in 2008 and 2009 I started working on Pyrolog again over the 2009 Christmas break. Since the stackless transformation wasn't compatible with the new JIT I had to switch to a different architecture, still used today. The first version of the Pyrolog architecture had already stored success continuations on the heap. The goal of this refactoring was to also store the failure continuations as reified RPython objects.

# the simplified main interpreter loop

def interpret(sc, fc, trail):
    while not sc.is_done():
        try:
            sc, fc, trail = sc.activate(fc, trail)
        except UnificationFailed:
            sc, fc, trail = fc.fail(trail)

This had the effect that the host stack use is completely flat when interpreting Prolog calls. The state needed to continue with Prolog execution is always stored in continuation objects on the heap. The interpreter is therefore dealing with two continuations, one activated upon success, and one on failure.

Additionally, there is a (segmented) trail object which stores the variable bindings that need to be undone when backtracking. Failure continuations have a reference to the part of the trail they need to backtrack to, when activated.

The downside of this approach is that the interpreter continuously has to allocate heap data for all the success and failure continuations. This puts a lot of pressure on the RPython GC. Also, term unification still uses the host stack, so unifying very deep terms can still be a problem and lead to implementation-level recursion errors.

JIT support

The real goal of the refactoring was to make the interpreter more JIT-friendly. Since the interpreter doesn't use recursion for Prolog calls any more, the loops at the Prolog level (expressed as recursion) can be traced by the JIT. All the continuous allocations of continuations can be optimized by the allocation removal of the RPython JIT. If all this works correctly, the performance of Pyrolog becomes very good, often beating Sicstus by a significant factor on suitable microbenchmarks.

Unfortunately this is mainly the case for code with very predictable control flow, ideally with nice loops. Since the main appeal of Prolog is to do backtracking search, control flow is typically not very predictable for interesting more Prolog-like programs. As soon as the control flow becomes too complicated and unpredictable, the continuations survive beyond the region that the JIT optimizes and have to be allocated on the heap again. Therefore Pyrolog has quite extreme performance cliffs.

We wrote a paper about this version of Pyrolog for PPDP 2010 [BLS2010] (together with Michael Leuschel and my later colleague David Schneider). That conference was super interesting and important for me. I met Paul Tarau (who invented BinProlog, one of the inspirations of Pyrolog. He later gave an excellent Prolog talk at the virtual machine summer school in 2016), Olivier Danvy (whose paper [BD2004] I should have cited. Genuinely one of the nicest people), Kenichi Asai (who had worked on partial evaluation and reflection) and several others. At that point during my PhD I was feeling relatively demotivated, and my chats with these researchers were incredibly encouraging.

On the bus trip to Gmunden, where the conference dinner took place, somebody explained how to implement unification of cyclic terms (i.e. rational trees) to me. Unfortunately I am not quite sure who it was any more, I think it might have been Thierry Martinez. This led to me implementing support for cyclic term unification. I still like that trick a lot and tried to explain it to Phil Zucker earlier this year. I hope to blog about it eventually. For a fun use case see my blog post on rational tree unification for dfa minimization.

2011/2012 Widening Prolog support

During 2011 and 2012 I supervised several Bachelor/Master theses that worked on adding features to Pyrolog. In the process we added support for Prolog modules, DCGs, attributed variables, freeze and when. All these were done by my excellent students Sven Hager and René Bartelmus. This left the fundamental execution model intact (with some small additions). I also spent some time improving the error messages of Pyrolog to for example show backtraces on Prolog exceptions.

Pyrolog also served as an early test case for David Schneider's ARM JIT backend. In January 2011, he could already run Pyrolog microbenchmarks with the JIT on a BeagleBoard, while the backend still needed debugging before running more complex Python benchmarks.

In 2012 I mostly wrote my PhD thesis [Bolz2012]. It contains a chapter on Pyrolog based on the PPDP 2010 paper. That chapter argues that the fact that Pyrolog works relatively well demonstrates that the RPython meta-JIT can be used for quite a wide variety of languages.

There was also another chapter that compared how a meta-tracing JIT works with more classical partial evaluation approaches. The code of that was again written in Prolog and originally presented here on the blog.

At this point I left Düsseldorf. In the winter semester 2012/13 I spent half a year in Berlin at Hasso-Plattner-Institute with my PhD co-advisor Robert Hirschfeld as a postdoc. In 2013 I started working with Laurie Tratt at King's College.

At this point the main Pyrolog story kind of ends. There were only some small improvements happening in the succeeding years. However, Pyrolog ideas led to followup research in other projects, and I want to discuss some of them.

2012/2013 Compressed Terms

One experiment that I had tried to get working in Pyrolog in 2010 and 2011, but ended up never merging, was compressed terms. The idea comes from the observation that certain patterns of terms occur repeatedly. For example an association list has terms of the form [A/B|C] repeated. Instead of allocating separate list and pair objects for every entry, the plan was to share a description of that structure, the "shape", and store each entry's keys and values in a flat storage data structure. This would make every entry have less indirection and use fewer words of memory.

However, I had never managed to get that working in Prolog, because variable bindings can create these patterns after the fact. A field that initially contained an unbound variable could later refer to a whole structured term, so the opportunities for compression changed after construction, and again on backtracking.

When I was at HPI for six months in winter 2012/2013 I worked with Tobias Pape to implement the same compression idea for a simpler functional language called lamb, later Theseus. Tobias pushed this approach much further in the following years [PBH2015], [PBH2017]. My hope was to eventually understand the problem well enough to be able to port it back to Pyrolog. But that part never happened. We did eventually try those ideas in Pycket though.

2013 Unipycation

In 2013 I started working in London. The goal of our research project there was "language composition". Edd Barrett, Laurie Tratt and I implemented combined RPython implementations of PyPy plus another language, with a way to integrate the two involved languages. The first pair we tried was combining PyPy and Pyrolog in a project called Unipycation [BBT2013]. The project made it possible to call into Prolog from Python and vice versa. The RPython meta-JIT can optimize across this language boundary and even perform cross-language inlining. Unfortunately we never found super compelling use-cases for this combination.

2013 Pycket

Also in 2013 I started working on Pycket, an RPython implementation of Racket, together with Sam Tobin-Hochstadt and Jeremy Siek. Later Tobias Pape and Spenser Bauman also joined. The Pycket architecture was indirectly inspired by the Pyrolog continuation approach. We implemented the CEK machine relatively directly in RPython. This also used a flat host stack and it made implementing things like call/cc relatively straightforward [Pycket2015].

One of the great new ideas we adopted in Pycket was "false loop filtering", originally from IBM Research [HWISN2011]. This takes calling context into account when tracing function calls and prevents the tracing JIT from recognizing "false loops", which would never run for more than one iteration. I always wanted to port this idea back to Pyrolog and implemented a prototype, but never really polished it enough to be able to merge it.

Another implementation technique in Pycket was to make continuation objects much smaller, to make the case of them escaping to the heap less bad. This approach did make it back to Pyrolog.

2022-2024 RPython implementation of Effekt

I left King's College in 2016 and have been back at Uni Düsseldorf (half-time) since 2017. In 2022 I started working with Marcial Gaißert and Jonathan Brachthäuser on an RPython implementation of Effekt and related languages. This was an implementation of a JIT for effect handlers in RPython [GBB2025]. Again, it uses the approach of having reified stack frames/continuations which can then be manipulated by the interpreter, adding effect handlers on top of it.

2026 Git Conversion

This fall, I got an email by a researcher about Pyrolog and realized that it was 20 years old this year. Subsequently I merged various strands of the surviving mercurial history into a new Github repo, fixed some bugs and implemented some convenience features.

Conclusion

Pyrolog has always been a much more researchy project than PyPy itself. While PyPy tries to be an open source project and "product", Pyrolog never had any users and even I never used it for anything beyond small demos, some research prototypes, and benchmarking. While some quite interesting research came out of Pyrolog, I think having some real applications running on top of it would have given me a much more concrete goal to improve it towards.

Implementation wise, Pyrolog inherited some of the problems of PyPy. In PyPy we were quite strongly focused on peak performance in the first few years, leading to performance cliffs. Pyrolog also had those in even stronger form, due to the constant allocations of the interpreter.

Some very interesting interaction between Pyrolog, various Prolog JIT prototypes and the PyPy/RPython meta-JIT emerged. Pyrolog forced the RPython JIT to support quite different execution models which made it applicable to other languages than just object-oriented imperative ones.

I still have no concrete use case for Pyrolog so it's kind of unlikely that I will work much more on it right now. But my development on this project was always quite bursty, so I am bound to return to it at some point in the future.

I would like to end with thanking all the contributors, my other collaborators and the long line of Prolog researchers that inspired and encouraged me.

Creating for yourself

Today marks the 7th anniversary of starting this blog. If you've been following along for a while: thank you! 😊

After observing Volunteer Responsibility Amnesty Day in 2025 and stopping most of my volunteer OSS contributions, I really leaned hard into using my creative leisure time "for me". This has helped protect my flame from burn-out and focus on areas I'm excited about, like writing, video-games (retro, modding, ...developing? 👀), and keeping my volunteer OSS focus on mostly a single project: truststore. This year was the first time since 2016 that I was uninvolved in the latest release of urllib3, which felt both deeply rewarding and strange as I read the v2.8.0 release notes.

Writing

This year I published 54 articles to my blog, an average of once per week across a year. This wasn't a specific goal I had in mind, looking through the past year of articles has me thinking "oh, that was this year??". Writing continued to be a focus of my role at the PSF, too, where I wrote 14 articles for Python Insider, 5 for the PSF blog, and 2 for the Python Package Index blog (that's a lot of yapping!)

The piece I'm most proud of from this year is “Food JPEGs in Super Smash Bros. and Kirby Air Riders”. This took tons of research, buying the Japanese Kirby Air Ride, writing some custom software, and I think this work really showed through in the end. This was my most-visited article of the year (mostly from the Hacker News front page).

My under-dog favorite is “Adding Animal Crossing events to your digital calendar” where I got to deep-dive into how Animal Crossing events work, differences between regions and games, the Animal Crossing decompilation project, and bought a physical Animal Crossing calendar too. I learned about the ICS calendar format and which features are supported across different platforms like iOS and Android.

Software

I took a much more fast-and-loose approach to sharing software I've worked on this year, opting to use this blog and Gists instead of each project needing a forge, issue tracker, and to be published to PyPI. I found that this approach let me spend more time on what actually mattered to me: being able to tell the story!

  • Adding Animal Crossing events to your digital calendar (Source)

  • sugarcookie: a sprite-sheet cutting tool (Source)

I continued building tools allowing you to extract and archive ROMs from legally owned physical copies of games:

  • Nintendo e-Reader cards
  • GC-to-GBA Joyboot, such as Billy Hatcher "Joycarry" games
  • Ocarina of Time: Master Quest Bonus Disc
  • GBA BIOS

Finally, I created tools that track my play activity across all the platforms I care about. Can't improve what you don't measure, this data comes up in the next section:

  • GameCube Play Activity (Memcard Pro GC) (Source)

Gaming

Ever since 2023 I've been “in my feels” about gaming. I considered myself a gamer, but I wasn't actually playing games. I set a goal to get back into the hobby more seriously, and figure out how I can integrate play back into my week. 2025 was successful already, and 2026 was even more so.

I ended up really experimenting this year by writing about a slice of my experience for four of the games I completed. I don't think I'll want to write about every game I play, but thinking deeply about my feelings while playing the game and especially the time between play sessions has been an interesting lens to look through. Maybe more to come here?

  • “The Legend of Zelda: Link’s Awakening” respects your time
  • The Frog for Whom the Bell Tolls
  • Bowser’s Fury outshines Sunshine, SA2B, and Billy Hatcher
  • Mario Kart World and “seamless” media

Calvin #1: PyPI's redesigned project pages now warn when provenance changes

  • Live now on pypi.org (Nov 6). Announced in July, shaped by user research, funded by OpenSSF and built by Kabu Creative.
  • New Security tab on every project page shows each release's provenance state and per-file attestations, with a link to the transparency log.
  • Two attestation types: PyPI Publish (uploaded by a Trusted Publisher) and SLSA Build Provenance (ties a file to its source commit, build platform and instructions).
  • "No provenance" isn't a malware flag; it means the file's origin can't be cryptographically verified. That's typical for older releases or projects without Trusted Publishing.
  • Four new provenance warnings: Incomplete (e.g. wheels via Trusted Publishing but sdist via API token), Inconsistent (mixed build systems or repos), Change (different repo or workflow than last release), Loss (stopped publishing attestations).
  • Warnings are meant to catch files that may not have come from the maintainers, such as after an account or publishing-workflow takeover. PyPI says a Change of Provenance "can also indicate a compromised pipeline or account takeover," and an unexpected Loss of Provenance should be treated "with skepticism until verified.”
  • Warnings now sit at the top of the page with yanked, archived and pre-release banners. A quarantine banner overrides all of them. Sidebar metadata is labeled "PyPI data" or "Data verified by PyPI."
  • Release files tab gets search, filters, a per-file provenance column and orange icons for "late" files (uploaded more than 14 days after release). Those only appear on releases from before July 2026, when PyPI started rejecting new files after 14 days.

Michael #2: gilguard catches the import that turns the GIL back on

gilguard catches a nasty free-threading surprise: importing a C extension without declared free-threading support can turn the GIL back on for the rest of the process. Import gilguard first and it raises a detailed error naming the extension, distribution/version, import chain, and the line of your code responsible.

  • A transitive dependency can undo your move to free-threaded Python. Fail at the offending import instead of discovering lost parallelism in a benchmark.
  • Uses import hooks plus CPython's warning path to catch GIL activation; GILEnabledError inherits BaseException, so even except Exception won't swallow it.
  • python -m gilguard audits installed top-level packages in fresh interpreters. Supports targeted modules and JSON output; exits 1 when it finds an offender for CI.
  • The audit misses extensions loaded lazily on first use. Exercise those paths in tests with gilguard installed early; one process can identify only its first GIL-flipping import.
  • GILGUARD_ACTION=warn reports without stopping; off permits standard builds. Default mode raises on standard Python or an already-enabled GIL. PYTHON_GIL=0 prevents activation, so the guard cannot detect offenders there.
  • Published on PyPI as 0.1.0. Install with uv pip install gilguard. Python 3.13+, MIT; no runtime dependencies.

Calvin #3: pip 26.3: pylock extras, and pip says plainly that untrusted wheels aren't safe

  • pip 26.3 released Nov 2. Its security policy now says pip "has never considered installing untrusted wheels safe," because Python's import system is dynamic, so pip can't guarantee installing a wheel won't execute third-party code (#14412).
  • The change: pip blocks unexpected imports during wheel installation "to avoid loading code from packages being replaced." It loads what it needs for installing and error reporting up front (PR #14247, merged Nov 2: https://github.com/pypa/pip/pull/14247).
  • CLI vs. library use: from the command line, imports stay blocked until pip exits. Code that calls pip programmatically gets warnings after control returns.
  • Rolled out in steps: pip 26.1 started warning ("Unexpected import of '...' after pip install started. pip 26.3 will enforce this behavior change"), and 26.3 turns it into an error. Tracking issue #13842 collected reports before enforcement: https://github.com/pypa/pip/issues/13842
  • What tripped it in the wild: a .pth startup hook at Pradyun Gedam's workplace that registers an atexit handler to report interpreter usage metrics over HTTP (https://github.com/pypa/pip/issues/13842#issuecomment-4613463933), plus warnings involving lark_oapi and rich._emoji_codes.
  • Escape-hatch debate: the author, Damian Shaw (notatallshaw), suggested appending module names to pip._internal.commands.install._EAGER_IMPORTS. Pradyun objected that this requires importing from pip._internal, that importing pip's install command is expensive (about 689 ms with no .pyc files and a cold filesystem cache), and that pip isn't necessarily installed in every environment. Shaw's reply: it "just moves where the import cost is," and turning the warning into an error is "crucial."
  • Interrupted installs: pip now writes recovery metadata and reports recovery failures and where it kept backups. An interrupted uninstall may need manual recovery, and an interrupted --target install can leave a partial target directory.
  • Also in 26.3: -r pylock.toml[extra1,extra2] for extras, --group pylock.toml:docs for non-default groups, --no-binary with pylock files, and pip refuses to follow an HTTP redirect to a file: URL.

Michael #4: All developer tools

HeyUtils is a collection of 306 free developer utilities in 41 categories, with no account or installation required. The full catalog covers data conversion, APIs, networking, DevOps, frontend work and binary protocols. Python developers get model generation and packaging helpers alongside the everyday formatters and decoders worth bookmarking.

  • One bookmark for the tiny jobs that interrupt coding: inspect a payload, compare configs, decode a token or figure out a cron schedule.
  • Data tools include JSON/YAML/TOML conversion, CSV joins, JSONPath and JMESPath testers, a jq playground, and a SQLite viewer with read-only queries.
  • Python-specific options go beyond regex: generate dataclasses or Pydantic models from JSON, check PEP 440 versions, and inspect requirements.txt or pyproject.toml dependencies.
  • The collection also covers JWT/OAuth debugging, DNS and TLS checks, Docker Compose, Kubernetes YAML, GitHub Actions, and frontend helpers such as color contrast checks.
  • Most tools process input locally, according to the site. Network tools make requests after you start them and explain what they send; don't assume every page is offline.
  • Generated models need review: sample data cannot describe a complete validation contract, and dataclass type hints do not validate incoming values.

AI and Irontown

For our last movie night we watched Princess Mononoke. I remembered loving that movie when I was younger, but watching it as an adult made me once again realize how Miyazaki has a way of expressing diverging sides without picking one. The film shows people, animals and spirits with legitimate and largely incompatible needs, and each faction is further engaged in infighting.

A central arc of the story is Lady Eboshi’s Irontown, which gives people who were largely rejected by society work, protection and dignity. She gives shelter and work to those who have leprosy and to former sex workers. The mine and the weapon factory provide them with a livelihood, but they also destroy the forest around them. The forest’s inhabitants, in particular the boars, response choose war against the humans to defend themselves. Eboshi is not portrayed as a villain. She does real good for the people of Irontown, but also is an arms manufacturer who is destroying the forest around it.

The film anchors around a character called Ashitaka, who has been condemned to death by an unfortunate encounter with a god that was turned into a demon after being fatally wounded by one of Eboshi’s guns. He doesn’t take sides, but he wants to understand and find the truth. Another character is San (whom they call Mononoke), a girl who was raised by wolves and, despite being human, considers herself part of the forest.

This movie’s backdrop is human progress, and it reminded me so much of the challenges we are facing today with AI. AI is useful, empowering and consequential beyond the people who benefit from it. It’s similar to the iron in the story. Irontown gives the outcast purpose and power. There are people today who see themselves in an environment just like that: people who were never let in before and who can now build things that were out of reach. They are wielding AI to their benefit. But there are negative externalities. In Mononoke it’s not just the spirits and animals in the forest who bear the consequences, it’s also other people. Irontown produces guns, and its people are engaged in fights with other humans. There is conflict and power seeking everywhere.

Another, unseen character is the power-hungry emperor, who wants the head of the forest spirit, a god, because he believes it will make him immortal. Eboshi is willing to support that cause, not because she thinks it’s right, but because she is at war with the forest’s gods who are preventing her expansion, so they have a common interest. But there really is no need for anyone to kill the forest spirit, in the same way there is no need for any one country to “win at AI”. What drives it is the fear of what happens if someone else wins first. And the emperor is willing to have a god killed for it. The people who help him with that desire, Eboshi included, don’t understand the chaos it will unleash.

There it also shows the dangers of seeking overwhelming power without comprehension of how to control its consequences. The pursuit of power itself becomes destructive. In the end, the gods die, and what grows back is not the old forest anymore. Irontown is destroyed. Eboshi wants to rebuild it, and to build a better town this time. Ashitaka stays with the people of Irontown after his curse is lifted by the dying forest spirit. San cannot forgive the humans and stays with the wolves. In the end, nothing is truly resolved, but there is a truce that came at great cost.

Ashitaka’s role in the movie is one of a neutral actor. He was wronged both by a spirit turned demon in the forest, who put a curse on him, and, indirectly, by Eboshi, who caused the spirit to turn evil. Yet he harbors no ill will towards either side. He sets out to “see with eyes unclouded by hate,” and in the course of the movie he is seen as an unaligned actor. He helps people on both sides, and stands in their way to prevent further conflict. He doesn’t feel like a pacifist, but he’s the closest to one there is. He’s a truth seeker in a changing world. Because he engages he’s seen as suspicious by both sides, and ends up shot by the people of Irontown when he tries to rescue San.

I use AI every day and find it genuinely empowering. But I also recognize the pain it causes some, and the moral problems it poses. It’s genuine progress for humanity, but it comes at significant cost. And many of those costs will be carried by people who aren’t going to benefit from it.

So it’s no surprise that AI feels like a threat to many today. Communities currently strongly align themselves with one side and fight against the other. The forest spirit did not have to die and the boars did not have to die in a senseless war.

The question is not whether there will be iron, but what kind of Irontown we build with it. I want our conversation about AI to make room for nuance and genuine understanding, for us to look at it and each other with eyes unclouded by hate. We should take advantage of its real benefits, but also recognize the damage it can do, and the choices we still have about what we build and what we’re willing to sacrifice for it. I like to think there is a path for people with differing opinions can find a way to live alongside before losing something we can’t get back.
